## Abstract

This study compares regularised logistic regression with four non-linear machine learning algorithms for the classification of chronic kidney disease (CKD) from routine clinical and laboratory markers, using the benchmark CKD dataset collected at Apollo Hospitals, India (N = 400; 250 CKD, 150 non-CKD; 24 predictors). The dataset presents three analytical difficulties: heavy, class-dependent missingness (0.25% to 38.0% per variable, with only 158 complete cases), a mixed-type feature space (11 continuous, 3 ordinal and 10 binary markers) and quasi-complete separation of the classes. Welch t-tests identified haemoglobin (t = -24.36, p = 3.61 × 10^-76^), urinary specific gravity (t = -22.21, p = 1.04 × 10^-67^) and packed cell volume (t = -21.15, p = 2.31 × 10^-61^) as the strongest continuous discriminators. Among the categorical markers, hypertension (χ² = 135.48) and diabetes mellitus (χ² = 121.26) were the strongest, and neither was recorded in any non-CKD patient. Haemoglobin, packed cell volume and red cell count were strongly collinear (r = 0.80 to 0.90), with variance inflation factors up to 4.48. To avoid optimistic bias, the data were partitioned before any preprocessing into a 70% training set (n = 280) and a 30% stratified holdout set (n = 120), and imputation, encoding, scaling, SMOTE-NC resampling and hyperparameter tuning were confined to the training portion of every fold of a repeated (3 × 10) stratified nested cross-validation. The unregularised logistic GLM failed under separation (standard errors up to 7.3 × 10^7^). Lasso-penalised logistic regression (C = 0.316), by contrast, retained 11 predictors and achieved 99.17% holdout accuracy (exact 95% CI [95.44%, 99.98%]), sensitivity 0.9867, specificity 1.0000, MCC 0.9825 and ROC-AUC 1.0000, with a Brier score of 0.0182 and directly interpretable odds ratios (haemoglobin OR = 0.18 per SD; albumin OR = 2.34 per SD; diabetes OR = 1.98). Random Forest, SVM-RBF, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest with SMOTE-NC achieved the same holdout accuracy (99.17%) and misclassified the same single patient, with Brier scores of 0.0060 to 0.0097; k-NN was the weakest classifier (92.50%). Because the holdout ROC curves were saturated, paired DeLong tests were undefined for identical AUCs and non-significant for the only estimable contrast (Lasso versus GBM: Z = 0.80, p = 0.42). Across the 30 cross-validation folds, the mean ROC-AUC of the Lasso model (0.9980, SD 0.0062) lay within 0.002 of the ensembles' (0.9994 to 1.0000), and variance-corrected paired t-tests detected no significant difference in discrimination between the Lasso model and any ensemble (all p ≥ 0.41). On routine renal and haematological markers, therefore, a regularised linear model attains diagnostic accuracy equal to that of the best non-linear ensembles while retaining the parametric transparency that they lack.

**Keywords:** Chronic Kidney Disease, Logistic Regression, Machine Learning, Odds Ratio, Explainable Artificial Intelligence.

## 1 Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a progressive and largely irreversible loss of renal function. An estimated 697.5 million people were affected in 2017, and the condition now ranks among the leading causes of death worldwide (GBD Chronic Kidney Disease Collaboration, 2020). Its prevalence of approximately one adult in ten is driven by the concurrent epidemics of hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Because the disease is typically silent until an advanced stage, most affected individuals remain undiagnosed until a substantial part of their renal reserve has been lost (Kovesdy, 2022). Diagnosis rests on a small panel of routine laboratory markers, principally serum creatinine (from which the estimated glomerular filtration rate is derived), albuminuria, haemoglobin and urinary indices, interpreted together with the patient's comorbidity history (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes [KDIGO] CKD Work Group, 2024). These markers are inexpensive, widely available and already collected in primary care, so the early classification of CKD from routine data constitutes a well-posed supervised learning problem of considerable clinical value.

Logistic Regression (LR) has served as the standard statistical model for binary clinical outcomes for more than half a century (Hosmer et al., 2013). Its enduring appeal lies in its parametric transparency. Each coefficient converts directly into an odds ratio (OR) with a confidence interval, which allows clinicians to inspect the direction and magnitude of every risk factor and satisfies the growing regulatory and ethical requirement that automated decisions be explainable (Rudin, 2019). This property is of particular importance in nephrology, where the physiological pathways linking anaemia, albuminuria, hypertension and diabetes to renal decline are well established, and a model whose coefficients contradicted them would justifiably be distrusted.

Standard LR nonetheless assumes that the log-odds of disease are an additive function of the predictors, an assumption that may not hold when markers interact or when the risk gradient is strongly non-linear (Hosmer et al., 2013). This limitation has motivated an extensive literature applying non-linear machine learning (ML) methods to CKD classification, including Random Forests (Breiman, 2001), gradient-boosted trees (Ogunleye & Wang, 2020), Support Vector Machines and k-Nearest Neighbours. Studies on the benchmark CKD dataset routinely report accuracies between 97% and 100% for these algorithms (Almasoud & Ward, 2019; Chittora et al., 2021; Debal & Sitote, 2022; Dritsas & Trigka, 2022; Elhoseny et al., 2019; Khan et al., 2020; Poonia et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2020; Senan et al., 2021), and a systematic review of ML in CKD identified tree ensembles as the family most frequently ranked first (Sanmarchi et al., 2023).

This development raises the familiar tension in clinical artificial intelligence between predictive accuracy and interpretability. An ensemble of several hundred trees operates as a black box whose internal reasoning cannot be audited at the bedside, and its gain in discrimination over regression is frequently small or absent when the comparison is conducted fairly (Christodoulou et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2026). Fair comparison, however, is precisely what much of the CKD literature lacks. Many published pipelines impute missing values or oversample the minority class before splitting the data, tune hyperparameters on the test set, or report point estimates without any measure of uncertainty; each of these practices is a documented source of leakage and optimistic bias (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023; Varoquaux, 2018). Few studies test whether the difference between a non-linear model and a regularised linear baseline is statistically distinguishable from zero, and fewer still assess probability calibration, although calibration is the property on which clinical risk communication depends (Van Calster et al., 2019). Current reporting standards for prediction models developed with regression or ML explicitly require these elements (Collins et al., 2024). In their absence, the literature cannot establish whether additional model complexity is clinically warranted.

The present study addresses these gaps by benchmarking regularised Logistic Regression against non-linear ML algorithms for CKD classification from routine clinical and laboratory markers, comparing the models on effectiveness, robustness, calibration and interpretability under a single leak-free protocol. This aim is pursued through five objectives:

1. To characterise the distributional properties, missingness structure and class proportions of the CKD clinical dataset.
2. To compare the numeric and categorical clinical markers between CKD and non-CKD patients using appropriate parametric and non-parametric inferential tests.
3. To identify the principal predictors of CKD using penalised parametric estimation (odds ratios) and non-parametric importance ranking (permutation importance and SHAP values).
4. To assess redundancy and multicollinearity among the haematological and renal markers and their consequences for the stability and interpretability of the parametric model.
5. To tune, cross-validate and compare Support Vector Machines, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting and k-Nearest Neighbours with Lasso and Elastic-Net Logistic Regression under a leak-free, repeated, stratified cross-validation protocol with an independent holdout set.

Beyond providing a further benchmark on a familiar dataset, the study makes a methodological and a clinical contribution. Methodologically, it shows that a mixed-type clinical dataset with up to 38% missingness per variable and quasi-complete class separation, a combination under which the unregularised logistic GLM fails outright (standard errors of order 10^7^), can be modelled satisfactorily with a Lasso-penalised logistic regression. The resulting model retains 11 of the 24 markers, achieves 99.17% holdout accuracy with a Brier score of 0.0182, and matches the accuracy of tuned Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and SVM classifiers under a protocol in which imputation, scaling, resampling and tuning never see the evaluation fold. Clinically, rather than stopping at a predicted label, the analysis identifies haemoglobin (t = -24.36), urinary specific gravity (t = -22.21) and packed cell volume (t = -21.15) as the primary quantitative markers of CKD and quantifies their diagnostic weight through interpretable odds ratios; each standard-deviation increase in haemoglobin, for example, multiplies the odds of CKD by 0.18, and each standard-deviation increase in urinary albumin multiplies them by 2.34. These estimates concur with the non-parametric importance rankings of the best ensemble.

The work is situated at the intersection of statistical learning and clinical nephrology. For practitioners, it supplies an empirical benchmark of classical regression against modern ensemble architectures on markers that any laboratory can provide, and it sets out explicit criteria for admitting transparent tools into primary renal screening. For methodologists, it contributes to explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in nephrology by showing how the same haematological, urinary and comorbidity signals are weighted within linear and non-linear frameworks, and by applying SHAP-based attributions to audit the best black-box model (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Lundberg & Lee, 2017).

The empirical evaluation uses the benchmark CKD dataset of 400 patient records and 24 clinical attributes originally collected at Apollo Hospitals, Tamil Nadu, India, and deposited in the UCI Machine Learning Repository (Rubini et al., 2015). The task is the binary classification of CKD versus non-CKD status; staging of CKD by estimated glomerular filtration rate lies outside the scope of the data. Attention is restricted to tabular classifier architectures so that the interpretability-accuracy trade-off can be isolated, and deep learning on imaging or longitudinal records is not considered. Model performance is evaluated by accuracy with exact binomial confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), ROC-AUC with DeLong confidence intervals and the Brier score, together with coefficient, odds-ratio and feature-importance analyses.

## 2 Methodology

This section describes the research design, the data, and the statistical and machine learning methods by which regularised Logistic Regression was compared with the non-linear algorithms for CKD classification.

The study adopts a comparative quantitative design in which the parametric logistic model is set against four non-parametric learners: Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), Support Vector Machines with a radial basis function kernel (SVM-RBF) and k-Nearest Neighbours (k-NN). Every predictor is a routinely measured laboratory value, a urinalysis grade or a recorded comorbidity; the predictor set therefore involves no clinical judgement, and the findings can be reproduced by anyone with access to the public data. Comparing the ensembles with a regularised linear baseline addresses directly whether their additional complexity yields a clinically meaningful improvement, while the repeated stratified cross-validation scheme both guards against overfitting and quantifies the sampling variability of every estimate. All analyses were performed in Python 3.15 using scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011) for preprocessing, model fitting and evaluation, imbalanced-learn (Lemaître et al., 2017) for SMOTE-NC resampling, statsmodels for the unregularised GLM and the variance inflation factors, SciPy for the inferential tests and SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) for local attribution. A single random seed (42) governed the data partition, the fold assignment, the bootstrap and every stochastic learner.

To prevent leakage, the data were partitioned before any imputation, encoding, scaling, resampling or model fitting into a 70% training set (n = 280; 175 CKD) and a 30% stratified holdout test set (n = 120; 75 CKD). All subsequent preprocessing was implemented as a scikit-learn pipeline and was therefore re-estimated from the training portion of every cross-validation fold: median imputation of continuous and ordinal markers, mode imputation of binary markers, standardisation (centring and scaling to unit variance) and, for the resampled Random Forest only, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling for mixed nominal and continuous data (SMOTE-NC; Chawla et al., 2002) with five neighbours. Hyperparameter selection was nested. An outer 10-fold stratified cross-validation, repeated three times to give 30 folds, measured out-of-sample performance, and within each outer training fold an inner 5-fold stratified grid search selected hyperparameters by ROC-AUC. The final model of each family was then re-tuned by 10-fold stratified cross-validation on the full training set and evaluated once on the holdout set. Because no modelling decision involved the holdout set, its estimates are unbiased with respect to tuning (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023; Rácz et al., 2021).

The study population comprises adult patients investigated for renal disease at Apollo Hospitals, Tamil Nadu, India, over a period of approximately two months in 2015, each represented by laboratory results and clinical history (Rubini et al., 2015). The sample of 400 observations (250 CKD, 150 non-CKD) was obtained by purposive sampling from the UCI Machine Learning Repository benchmark (ID 336) through its public Kaggle mirror. Each record contains 24 attributes. Eleven are continuous markers: age, blood pressure, random blood glucose, blood urea, serum creatinine, sodium, potassium, haemoglobin, packed cell volume, white cell count and red cell count. Three are ordinal urinalysis grades: specific gravity in five steps from 1.005 to 1.025, albumin from 0 to 5 and sugar from 0 to 5. Ten are binary markers: red blood cells normal or abnormal, pus cells normal or abnormal, pus cell clumps, bacteria, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, appetite, pedal oedema and anaemia. Binary markers were encoded as 1 for the clinically adverse level and 0 otherwise, and ordinal grades were retained as numeric scores. Known defects of the public file (stray tab characters, a leading space in one diabetes entry and tab-and-question-mark tokens in three numeric columns) were corrected before analysis, and the corrected data are released with the code.

The analysis proceeded in three phases: exploratory and inferential statistics with principal component analysis (PCA) and t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE); specification and tuning of the candidate models; and cross-validated evaluation, extended with paired DeLong testing, variance-corrected paired comparisons across folds, assessment of probability calibration, permutation and SHAP importance, partial dependence modelling, clinical threshold optimisation and a sensitivity analysis of the imputation strategy.

In the first phase, the missingness of every variable was tabulated overall and by class, since values that are missing because a test was not ordered are not missing completely at random and may themselves be informative (Rubin, 1976). Continuous and ordinal markers were summarised and compared between CKD and non-CKD patients on the available cases using Welch's t-test, which accommodates unequal variances:

$$t = \frac{\bar{X}_1 - \bar{X}_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}, \tag{1}$$

where $\bar{X}_1$ and $\bar{X}_2$ are the sample means, $s_1^2$ and $s_2^2$ the sample variances and $n_1$ and $n_2$ the group sizes of the CKD and non-CKD cohorts respectively, with Welch-Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Because several laboratory values are heavily right-skewed, each Welch test was accompanied by a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test as a distribution-free check. Binary markers were compared using Pearson's chi-square test of independence on the 2 × 2 table with Yates' continuity correction,

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2} \frac{(|O_{ij} - E_{ij}| - 0.5)^2}{E_{ij}}, \tag{2}$$

where $O_{ij}$ and $E_{ij}$ denote the observed and expected counts in cell $(i, j)$. Fisher's exact test was applied alongside, and the sample odds ratio was computed with a 0.5 continuity correction wherever a cell count was zero. Linear redundancy among the encoded predictors was evaluated with the Pearson correlation coefficient,

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})(Y_i - \bar{Y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \bar{X})^2}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_i - \bar{Y})^2}}, \tag{3}$$

where $X_i$ and $Y_i$ are the measurements of two predictors for observation $i$, computed on pairwise-complete observations, and with the variance inflation factor of each predictor,

$$\mathrm{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}, \tag{4}$$

where $R_j^2$ is the coefficient of determination from regressing predictor $j$ on all other predictors. PCA was performed on the standardised, median-imputed predictor matrix, and a two-dimensional t-SNE embedding (perplexity 30, PCA initialisation) was computed on the same matrix to visualise any non-linear class structure.

In the second phase, seven classifiers were specified. The reference model is the ordinary binomial generalised linear model (GLM) with a logit link,

$$P(Y = 1 \mid \mathbf{X}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_k X_k)}}, \tag{5}$$

where $\beta_0$ is the intercept and $\beta_j$ the log-odds coefficient of predictor $X_j$. When the classes can be separated by a hyperplane in the predictor space, the maximum likelihood estimates of this model do not exist and the fitted coefficients diverge (Albert & Anderson, 1984). Penalised Logistic Regression was therefore implemented by minimising the negative log-likelihood under an Elastic-Net penalty (Zou & Hastie, 2005),

$$\min_{\beta_0, \boldsymbol{\beta}} \left\{ -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[ y_i(\beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}) - \log\left(1 + e^{\beta_0 + \mathbf{x}_i^{\top}\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right) \right] + \lambda\left[ \alpha \sum_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_j| + \frac{1 - \alpha}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2 \right] \right\}, \tag{6}$$

where $\lambda$ controls the overall degree of shrinkage and $\alpha$ mixes the $\ell_1$ (Lasso) and $\ell_2$ (ridge) components. Setting $\alpha = 1$ recovers the Lasso of Tibshirani (1996), which performs automatic variable selection by shrinking some coefficients exactly to zero. In the scikit-learn parameterisation, $\lambda$ is expressed through its inverse $C = 1/(n\lambda)$. The Lasso model was tuned over nine values of $C$ from 10^-2^ to 10^2^, and the Elastic-Net model over $C \in \{10^{-2}, \ldots, 10^{2}\}$ and $\alpha \in \{0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$. Coefficients are reported per standard deviation of the standardised predictor and exponentiated to odds ratios, with 95% percentile confidence intervals obtained from 500 bootstrap resamples of the training set in which the entire pipeline, including imputation and standardisation, was refitted.

To capture non-linear decision boundaries, a Support Vector Machine with a radial basis function kernel was trained, in which the similarity between two standardised observations is

$$K(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \exp\left(-\gamma \lVert \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j \rVert^2\right), \tag{7}$$

with $\gamma$ the kernel width and $C$ the margin penalty. These were tuned over $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10, 100\}$ and $\gamma \in \{\text{scale}, 0.01, 0.1\}$, and class probabilities were obtained by Platt scaling within the training folds. A Random Forest of 500 trees (Breiman, 2001) was tuned over the number of candidate features per split and the minimum leaf size, and a Gradient Boosting Machine over the number of trees, the learning rate and the tree depth. A second Random Forest was trained on SMOTE-NC-resampled training folds to determine whether synthetic balancing of the 62.5 : 37.5 class ratio improved sensitivity. Finally, k-NN classified each patient by majority vote among its $k$ nearest training neighbours, with $k$ tuned over $\{3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15\}$ and distance measured in the standardised space by

$$d(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{q}) = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{p}(p_j - q_j)^2}, \tag{8}$$

where $p_j$ and $q_j$ are the standardised values of predictor $j$ for the two patients.

In the third phase, model performance was evaluated at two levels: out-of-fold performance across the 30 outer folds of the repeated nested cross-validation on the training set, and a single evaluation on the untouched 30% holdout set (n = 120). Efficacy was measured by classification accuracy with exact Clopper-Pearson 95% binomial confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC; Hanley & McNeil, 1982), each accompanied by its standard deviation and 95% confidence interval across folds. The MCC was included because it is recommended for imbalanced classes, being high only when all four cells of the confusion matrix are favourable (Chicco & Jurman, 2020). Differences between correlated holdout ROC curves were tested with DeLong's paired test (DeLong et al., 1988),

$$Z = \frac{\mathrm{AUC}_1 - \mathrm{AUC}_2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{AUC}_1) + \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{AUC}_2) - 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\mathrm{AUC}_1, \mathrm{AUC}_2)}}, \tag{9}$$

where the variances and covariance are estimated from the placement values of the two models. Because the DeLong statistic is undefined when both curves are perfect, pairwise differences in out-of-fold ROC-AUC were additionally tested across the 30 cross-validation folds with the paired t-test of Nadeau and Bengio (2003), whose variance is inflated to account for the overlap between training sets,

$$t = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{1}{K} + \dfrac{n_{\text{test}}}{n_{\text{train}}}\right)\hat{\sigma}_d^2}}, \tag{10}$$

where $\bar{d}$ and $\hat{\sigma}_d^2$ are the mean and variance of the $K = 30$ fold-wise differences and $n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}} = 1/9$ for 10-fold partitions. The reliability of the predicted probabilities was measured on the holdout set by the Brier score,

$$\mathrm{BS} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{p}_i - y_i)^2, \tag{11}$$

where $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted probability of CKD and $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ the observed status (Steyerberg et al., 2010), together with reliability diagrams, the expected calibration error over quintile bins and Spiegelhalter's z-test of calibration (Spiegelhalter, 1986). Variable importance in the best ensemble was assessed in three ways: by Gini impurity decrease, by permutation importance on the holdout set (50 permutations, loss in ROC-AUC) and by mean absolute SHAP values on the training set (Lundberg & Lee, 2017). Partial dependence curves were drawn over the observed range of the four most influential markers. Because a missed case of CKD is costlier than a false alarm in renal screening, the decision threshold was varied over $p_t \in [0.05, 0.95]$ and the resulting false-negative counts were tabulated. Finally, the sensitivity of the conclusions to the imputation strategy was examined by repeating the Lasso and Random Forest pipelines with k-nearest-neighbour imputation (k = 5) and with chained-equation iterative imputation (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011) in place of median and mode imputation.
