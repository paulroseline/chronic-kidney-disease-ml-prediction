## 3 Data Application and Results

This section reports the results of applying the statistical and machine learning methods of Section 2 to the data. The dataset was collected at Apollo Hospitals, Tamil Nadu, India, donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository as dataset 336 (Rubini et al., 2015), and retrieved for this study from its Kaggle mirror at https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mansoordaku/ckdisease. It comprises 400 patient records, of which 250 are CKD cases (62.50%) and 150 non-CKD cases (37.50%). Stratification preserved this class distribution exactly in both the training split (175 of 280, 62.50%) and the holdout split (75 of 120, 62.50%).

### 3.1 Missingness Profile and Descriptive Statistics

In contrast to image-derived benchmarks, the CKD data are substantially incomplete. Of the 400 records, 242 (60.50%) lack at least one value and only 158 (39.50%) are complete. Table 1 and Figure 1 profile the missingness of each attribute. The proportion of missing values ranges from 0.25% for appetite, pedal oedema and anaemia to 38.00% for the red blood cell morphology result, and red cell count (32.75%), white cell count (26.50%), potassium (22.00%) and sodium (21.75%) are each missing in more than one-fifth of patients. Missingness is also strongly class-dependent. The red blood cell result is absent in 57.20% of CKD patients but in only 6.00% of non-CKD patients, the red cell count in 49.60% against 4.67%, and the white cell count in 39.60% against 4.67%, whereas the comorbidity fields (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease) are complete for every CKD patient. This pattern is consistent with tests that were not performed, or not transcribed, for patients already under renal care, and it violates the missing-completely-at-random assumption (Rubin, 1976). Because imputation must not borrow information from evaluation folds, and because the missingness pattern itself carries class information, all imputation was performed within the cross-validation pipeline on training folds only, and no missingness indicators were supplied to any model.

Table 2 summarises the 11 continuous and 3 ordinal markers on the available cases. Several laboratory values are heavily right-skewed. Serum creatinine has a median of 1.30 mg/dL but a mean of 3.07 mg/dL and a maximum of 76.00 mg/dL (skewness 7.48); blood urea has a median of 42.00 mg/dL against a mean of 57.43 mg/dL (skewness 2.62); and random blood glucose has a median of 121.00 mg/dL against a mean of 148.04 mg/dL (skewness 2.00). Haemoglobin (mean 12.53 g/dL, SD 2.91) and packed cell volume (mean 38.88%, SD 8.99) are approximately symmetric and span the full clinical range from severe anaemia to normal.

Table 3 reports the prevalence of each adverse binary marker by class. The most notable feature is that none of the ten adverse findings occurs in any non-CKD patient. Hypertension is present in 147 of 250 CKD patients (58.80%) and in 0 of 148 non-CKD patients; diabetes mellitus in 137 of 250 (54.80%) against 0 of 148; abnormal red blood cells in 47 of the 107 CKD patients with a result (43.93%) against 0 of 141; and abnormal pus cells in 76 of 194 (39.18%) against 0 of 141. Poor appetite (32.80%), pedal oedema (30.40%) and anaemia (24.00%) show the same one-sided pattern. The presence of any of these markers therefore excludes non-CKD status, a configuration that is highly informative for classification but, as Section 3.4 demonstrates, fatal for maximum likelihood estimation of an unpenalised logistic model.

The class-stratified densities in Figure 2 show clear separation across the principal renal and haematological markers. Serum creatinine in non-CKD patients is tightly concentrated below 1.3 mg/dL (mean 0.87 mg/dL), whereas in CKD patients it follows a broad, right-shifted distribution with a mean of 4.41 mg/dL and a long tail. ### 3.2 Correlation, Multicollinearity and Dimensional Structure

The Pearson correlation matrix of the 24 encoded predictors (Figure 3) reveals moderate to strong linear relationships among the haematological markers, and Table 4 itemises the 15 predictor pairs with |r| ≥ 0.60. The strongest association is between haemoglobin and packed cell volume (r = 0.8954, n = 327 pairs), followed by haemoglobin and red cell count (r = 0.7989) and packed cell volume and red cell count (r = 0.7916); all three quantify the circulating red cell mass, so this degree of association is physiologically expected. Random blood glucose and the urinary sugar grade are correlated at r = 0.7178, serum creatinine and sodium at r = -0.6902, and hypertension and diabetes at r = 0.6070. Unlike the geometric measurements of imaging-derived benchmarks, no pair in this dataset exceeds |r| = 0.90, so the collinearity is physiological rather than definitional.

{{TABLE:table04_correlated_pairs|Table 4: Predictor Pairs with Absolute Pearson Correlation of at Least 0.60}}

Variance inflation factors (Table 5) quantify the consequences for a linear model. The largest values belong to haemoglobin (VIF = 4.48, tolerance 0.22) and packed cell volume (VIF = 4.32), followed by serum creatinine (2.45), blood urea (2.39), hypertension (2.38) and albumin (2.26). All remaining predictors have VIF below 2.25, the smallest being white cell count (1.14). As no VIF reaches the conventional threshold of 5, multicollinearity is moderate and would not, on its own, destabilise a logistic regression. The instability documented in Section 3.4 therefore arises from class separation rather than from redundancy among the predictors.

To assess whether linear and non-linear projections resolve the class structure, PCA and t-SNE were applied to the standardised, median-imputed predictor matrix. The first principal component accounts for 28.98% of the total variance and the second for a further 7.94%, so that two components summarise 36.92% and six components 59.14% (Table 6). The first component can be read as a general axis of renal health, loading positively on haemoglobin (0.313), packed cell volume (0.314), red cell count (0.283) and specific gravity (0.229) and negatively on hypertension (-0.273), albumin (-0.256), blood urea (-0.246) and diabetes (-0.242). In the PCA biplot (Figure 4), the 150 non-CKD patients form a compact cluster at PC1 scores above approximately 1.5, whereas CKD patients spread across the negative half of the first component and along the second, which contrasts glycaemic markers (blood glucose and sugar, with loadings of 0.40 and 0.42) with azotaemic markers (blood urea and creatinine, -0.30 and -0.38). The t-SNE embedding (Figure 5) resolves the two classes into two well-separated manifolds with a narrow interface. The data thus contain a strong and predominantly linear class signal, with a small number of CKD cases lying close to the non-CKD region.

### 3.3 Inferential Feature Evaluation and Statistical Significance

Table 7 reports the Welch t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test for each continuous and ordinal marker. Thirteen of the 14 markers differ significantly between classes at α = 0.05 under both tests. The strongest discriminator is haemoglobin (difference -4.54 g/dL, t = -24.36, df = 335.5, p = 3.61 × 10^-76^, 95% CI [-4.91, -4.17]), followed by urinary specific gravity (t = -22.21, p = 1.04 × 10^-67^), packed cell volume (difference -13.40%, t = -21.15, p = 2.31 × 10^-61^), urinary albumin (t = 18.14, p = 9.99 × 10^-45^) and red cell count (difference -1.43 million/cmm, t = -15.62, p = 2.13 × 10^-37^). Random blood glucose (difference 67.70 mg/dL, t = 10.40) and blood urea (difference 39.59 mg/dL, t = 10.09) follow. Serum creatinine, despite a mean difference of 3.55 mg/dL, ranks only ninth by t-statistic (t = 7.86, p = 1.31 × 10^-13^) because its CKD distribution is so heavily skewed; its Mann-Whitney statistic (U = 31,841.5, p = 6.58 × 10^-44^) confirms a near-complete ordinal separation. Potassium is the only marker that does not discriminate (difference 0.54 mEq/L, t = 1.60, p = 0.112; Mann-Whitney p = 0.788). Because albumin and sugar are identically zero in every non-CKD patient, their Welch tests reduce to one-sample tests on the CKD group (df = 208 and 205), a further manifestation of the class separation.

{{TABLE:table07_welch_mannwhitney|Table 7: Welch t-Tests and Mann-Whitney U Tests Comparing Continuous and Ordinal Markers between CKD and Non-CKD Patients}}

Table 8 reports the chi-square and Fisher exact tests for the ten binary markers, all of which are significant. Hypertension (χ² = 135.48, Fisher p = 1.05 × 10^-40^) and diabetes mellitus (χ² = 121.26, p = 4.68 × 10^-37^) are the strongest categorical predictors, followed by abnormal red blood cells (χ² = 73.58), abnormal pus cells (χ² = 69.23), poor appetite (χ² = 59.52), pedal oedema (χ² = 54.00) and anaemia (χ² = 40.23); bacteria is the weakest (χ² = 11.98, Fisher p = 4.04 × 10^-5^). Because every adverse finding has a zero cell among non-CKD patients, the sample odds ratios were computed with a 0.5 continuity correction and are extremely large, with wide intervals (hypertension OR = 423.26, 95% CI [26.05, 6876.12]; diabetes OR = 359.80, 95% CI [22.15, 5843.75]). The minimum expected cell count is at least 8.1 for every table, so the chi-square approximation is adequate, and the Fisher tests agree in every case.

### 3.4 Model Estimation, Regularisation and Parameter Interpretability

Figure 6 traces the Lasso regularisation path on the training set. Cross-validated ROC-AUC rises from 0.500 at the strongest penalty (C = 0.01, at which all coefficients are zero) to above 0.99 by C = 0.0316 and reaches its maximum of 1.0000 at C = 0.316, the value selected by 10-fold cross-validation, before declining slightly at weaker penalties. The coefficient profile explains why the plateau is not flat in practice: as the penalty is relaxed beyond the selected value, the coefficients of specific gravity, packed cell volume and haemoglobin diverge towards -12, -8 and -4 standard-deviation units respectively, the characteristic signature of a likelihood without a finite maximum.

{{FIG:fig06_lasso_path|Figure 6: Cross-Validated ROC-AUC across the L1 Penalty Grid (Left) and Coefficient Shrinkage Profile of the Standardised Predictors (Right)}}

Table 9 lists the Lasso coefficients at the selected penalty, their odds ratios per standard deviation and the 95% percentile bootstrap intervals. The Lasso retained 11 of the 24 predictors and set the remaining 13, including serum creatinine, blood urea, age, blood pressure, sodium, potassium and white cell count, exactly to zero. In descending order of magnitude, the retained markers are haemoglobin (β = -1.7403, OR = 0.1755), specific gravity (β = -1.6326, OR = 0.1954), albumin (β = 0.8493, OR = 2.3380), diabetes mellitus (β = 0.6829, OR = 1.9797), hypertension (β = 0.5749, OR = 1.7770), poor appetite (OR = 1.3212), pedal oedema (OR = 1.2960), red cell count (OR = 0.8067), random blood glucose (OR = 1.1996), packed cell volume (OR = 0.8414) and sugar (OR = 1.0958). Every direction is physiologically coherent: each standard-deviation increase in haemoglobin lowers the odds of CKD by a factor of 5.7, each standard-deviation increase in urinary albumin raises them 2.3-fold, and the presence of diabetes or hypertension approximately doubles them after adjustment for the laboratory markers. The exclusion of serum creatinine and blood urea, the two markers a clinician would name first, reflects redundancy rather than irrelevance. Their information is already carried by haemoglobin, specific gravity and albumin, which separate the classes more cleanly, and their heavy skewness makes them weak linear predictors on the standardised scale. The 500-replicate bootstrap in Table 9 quantifies the stability of this selection. Haemoglobin, specific gravity and albumin were retained in every replicate, and their percentile intervals exclude an odds ratio of 1 (haemoglobin 95% CI [0.10, 0.39]; specific gravity [0.14, 0.29]; albumin [1.48, 3.36]). Diabetes was retained in 98.8% of replicates (95% CI [1.12, 2.91]) and hypertension in 97.0% (95% CI [1.00, 2.69]), whereas the six weaker markers were retained in only 60% to 79% of replicates and their intervals include 1. The Elastic-Net model (C = 0.1, α = 0.2) retained 19 predictors with the same five leading markers (specific gravity OR = 0.374, haemoglobin OR = 0.469, albumin OR = 1.746, hypertension OR = 1.665, diabetes OR = 1.638), but its shrinkage was more diffuse and it performed no better in cross-validation; the sparser Lasso model is therefore carried forward as the parametric reference.

{{TABLE:table09_lasso_coefficients_or|Table 9: Lasso Logistic Regression Coefficients, Odds Ratios per Standard Deviation and 95% Bootstrap Confidence Intervals}}

Table 10 demonstrates why regularisation was necessary. It reports the unpenalised binomial GLM fitted by maximum likelihood on the same standardised training data. The fit did not converge, and statsmodels issued repeated warnings of perfect separation. The estimated log-odds coefficients are of implausible magnitude (specific gravity -50.95, serum creatinine 57.61, packed cell volume -35.07, albumin 32.23), their standard errors range from 1.2 × 10^6^ to 7.3 × 10^7^, four standard errors (including that of the intercept) could not be computed because the information matrix is singular, every Wald z-statistic is effectively zero and every p-value equals 1.000. The model separates the training data perfectly (training AUC = 1.0000) but conveys no inferential information: no coefficient is distinguishable from zero and no odds ratio can be interpreted. This is the textbook outcome of quasi-complete separation (Albert & Anderson, 1984), for which penalisation, or alternatively Firth's bias-reduced likelihood (Firth, 1993), is the standard remedy. Figure 7 presents the Lasso odds ratios and bootstrap intervals as a forest plot, which makes evident the protective gradient of the haematological and urinary concentration markers and the risk gradient of albuminuria and the two comorbidities.

### 3.5 Cross-Validation Uncertainty and Holdout Test Set Evaluation

Performance was assessed at two levels: (1) out-of-fold performance across the 30 folds of the repeated nested cross-validation on the training set, with standard deviations and 95% confidence intervals (Table 12 and Figure 8), and (2) a single final evaluation on the untouched 30% holdout set (n = 120; Table 13, Figure 9 and Figure 10). Table 11 records the hyperparameter grid searched for each architecture, the values selected by 10-fold cross-validation on the full training set and the corresponding inner cross-validated ROC-AUC. The selected configurations are parsimonious: Lasso C = 0.316, Elastic-Net C = 0.1 with α = 0.2, k = 9 neighbours, a weakly regularised SVM (C = 0.1, γ = scale), Random Forests with √p candidate features and unrestricted leaves, and a shallow Gradient Boosting Machine of 100 depth-2 trees with learning rate 0.05.

{{TABLE:table11_hyperparameters|Table 11: Hyperparameter Grids, Selected Values and Inner 10-Fold Cross-Validated ROC-AUC for Each Classifier}}

Table 12 and Figure 8 summarise the out-of-fold results. Across the 30 folds, SVM-RBF and Random Forest achieved a mean ROC-AUC of 1.0000 with no fold-to-fold variation; Random Forest with SMOTE-NC averaged 0.9998 (SD 0.0010), Elastic-Net 0.9995 (SD 0.0022) and Gradient Boosting 0.9994 (SD 0.0022). The Lasso averaged 0.9980 (SD 0.0062, 95% CI [0.9957, 1.0000], minimum fold 0.9679), so the ensembles' advantage in discrimination is smaller than the Lasso's own fold-to-fold standard deviation and lies within its confidence interval. k-NN was the least stable architecture (mean 0.9913, SD 0.0136, minimum 0.9679). Out-of-fold accuracy followed a similar pattern: SVM-RBF 0.9988 (SD 0.0065), Random Forest with SMOTE-NC 0.9929 (SD 0.0145), Random Forest 0.9917 (SD 0.0154), Gradient Boosting 0.9798 (SD 0.0292), Elastic-Net 0.9786 (SD 0.0259), Lasso 0.9726 (SD 0.0307) and k-NN 0.9345 (SD 0.0470). The folds in which the Lasso lost accuracy were those in which it missed CKD cases (mean sensitivity 0.9562); it never produced a false positive (specificity 1.0000). The threshold analysis in Section 3.9 shows this to be a consequence of the default 0.5 cut-off rather than of the model's ranking of patients. The out-of-fold Brier scores favoured the ensembles (SVM-RBF 0.0038, Random Forest with SMOTE-NC 0.0101, Random Forest 0.0107, Gradient Boosting 0.0125) over the Lasso (0.0264), Elastic-Net (0.0401) and k-NN (0.0439).

Lasso Logistic Regression, SVM-RBF, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting and Random Forest with SMOTE-NC all achieved an identical accuracy of 99.17% (exact 95% CI [95.44%, 99.98%]), classifying 119 of 120 patients correctly with sensitivity 0.9867, specificity 1.0000, precision 1.0000, F1-score 0.9933 and MCC 0.9825. ### 3.6 Paired DeLong Testing and Variance-Corrected Fold-Wise Comparisons

To test formally whether any apparent difference in discrimination between the Lasso model and the non-linear learners was statistically significant, paired DeLong tests were performed on the holdout predictions (Table 14). Because the ROC curves of six of the seven classifiers were perfect on the holdout set, their AUC difference and its variance are both exactly zero, and the DeLong statistic of Equation 9 is undefined for those pairs; these contrasts are reported as not significant by construction. The only estimable contrast involving the Lasso model is that with Gradient Boosting (AUC 1.0000 versus 0.9996), which yielded Z = 0.8044 (p = 0.4212); the same statistic is obtained for Random Forest versus Gradient Boosting. There is therefore no evidence of any difference in discriminative capacity between the regularised linear model and the non-linear ensembles on the holdout set.

{{TABLE:table14_delong|Table 14: DeLong Paired Tests of Holdout ROC-AUC between Lasso Logistic Regression and Each Non-Linear Classifier}}

Since a saturated holdout set cannot discriminate between models, the comparison was repeated across the 30 cross-validation folds using the variance-corrected paired t-test of Equation 10 (Table 15). The mean fold-wise ROC-AUC difference between the Lasso model and the ensembles was -0.0020 for SVM-RBF and for Random Forest (corrected t = -0.83, p = 0.412), -0.0018 for Random Forest with SMOTE-NC (t = -0.74, p = 0.465) and -0.0014 for Gradient Boosting (t = -0.55, p = 0.589). The Lasso was likewise not significantly different from Elastic-Net (p = 0.533) or k-NN (difference +0.0068, t = 1.13, p = 0.266). In no contrast did the corrected test reject the null hypothesis of equal discrimination at α = 0.05. Applied to the secondary metrics, the same procedure did detect differences on the probability scale. The Lasso's out-of-fold Brier score was significantly higher than that of SVM-RBF (difference 0.0227, t = 3.06, p = 0.005), Random Forest (0.0158, t = 2.07, p = 0.047) and Random Forest with SMOTE-NC (0.0164, t = 2.18, p = 0.038), and its accuracy was significantly lower than that of SVM-RBF (difference -0.0262, t = -2.22, p = 0.034) but not than that of any other model. The variance correction is important in this setting: the naive paired t-test, which treats the 30 overlapping folds as independent, is known to be badly anti-conservative (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003; Varoquaux, 2018) and would overstate the evidence against the linear model. On the available evidence, the discrimination of the tuned ensembles and of the Lasso Logistic Regression is statistically indistinguishable, and the ensembles' measurable advantage is confined to the sharpness of their probabilities.

### 3.7 Probability Calibration and Brier Score Assessment

In renal screening, well-calibrated probabilities are required to communicate risk and to set referral thresholds, so classification accuracy alone is insufficient (Van Calster et al., 2019). Table 16 reports the holdout Brier score, the expected calibration error over quintile bins and Spiegelhalter's z-test for each model, and Figure 11 shows the corresponding reliability diagrams. SVM-RBF achieved the lowest Brier score (0.0060), followed by Random Forest (0.0082), Random Forest with SMOTE-NC (0.0083) and Gradient Boosting (0.0097); the Lasso (0.0182) and Elastic-Net (0.0223) models were intermediate, and k-NN was clearly the worst (0.0471). The expected calibration error follows the same ordering (SVM-RBF 0.0106, Gradient Boosting 0.0135, Random Forest with SMOTE-NC 0.0213, Random Forest 0.0253, Lasso 0.0496, Elastic-Net 0.0706, k-NN 0.0796). Spiegelhalter's statistic, which is negative when predicted probabilities lie systematically too close to 0.5, detected no significant miscalibration for SVM-RBF (z = -1.04, p = 0.298), Gradient Boosting (z = -1.39, p = 0.164) or k-NN (z = 1.85, p = 0.064); borderline under-confidence for the two Random Forests (z = -2.14, p = 0.032; z = -1.97, p = 0.049); and clear under-confidence for the Lasso (z = -2.66, p = 0.008) and Elastic-Net (z = -3.16, p = 0.002) models, whose mean predicted probability of CKD (0.605 and 0.607) fell short of the observed proportion (0.625). Nevertheless, the reliability curves of the five best models lie close to the diagonal across all quintiles. The linear models are under-confident in the upper-middle range (mean predicted probability 0.84 in the fourth Lasso bin against an observed CKD proportion of 1.00), which is the expected effect of coefficient shrinkage. The k-NN curve is pathological: its lowest-probability bin (mean predicted 0.11) contains an observed CKD proportion of 1.00, because the nine CKD patients it misclassified received probabilities between 0.1 and 0.4 from their predominantly non-CKD neighbourhoods. This result reverses the finding on imaging-derived benchmarks that a penalised linear model is the best-calibrated classifier. In the present data, the quasi-separable structure allows the margin-based and tree-based learners to assign probabilities near 0 and 1 with justification, whereas shrinkage pulls the Lasso probabilities away from the extremes and inflates its Brier score even though its ranking of patients is perfect.

### 3.8 Feature Importance, Partial Dependence and SHAP Attribution

Table 17 compares three non-parametric importance measures for the tuned Random Forest with the parametric ranking of the Lasso. By Gini impurity decrease, haemoglobin (0.2000) is the dominant predictor, followed by serum creatinine (0.1428), packed cell volume (0.1283), specific gravity (0.1186), red cell count (0.0989), albumin (0.0658) and hypertension (0.0511). Mean absolute SHAP values on the training set rank the same seven markers in nearly the same order (haemoglobin 0.0917, serum creatinine 0.0775, specific gravity 0.0769, packed cell volume 0.0560, red cell count 0.0455, albumin 0.0453, hypertension 0.0356, diabetes 0.0275), and the ten weakest markers each contribute less than 0.005. Permutation importance on the holdout set is small in absolute terms for every variable (the largest loss in ROC-AUC is 0.0091 for specific gravity, followed by haemoglobin 0.0068, albumin 0.0028 and serum creatinine 0.0019). When the holdout AUC is 1.0000 and several markers carry redundant signal, permuting any single marker rarely alters a ranking; the measure is nonetheless consistent in placing the urinary and haematological markers first. The principal discrepancy between the parametric and non-parametric rankings concerns serum creatinine, which the Random Forest ranks second but the Lasso excluded. The forest exploits the sharp non-linear step in creatinine at approximately 1.2 to 1.5 mg/dL, whereas on a linear standardised scale the same variable is dominated by its extreme values and adds little once haemoglobin and specific gravity are in the model.

{{TABLE:table16_feature_importance|Table 17: Gini, Permutation and Mean Absolute SHAP Importance of Each Predictor in the Tuned Random Forest}}

Figure 12 presents the partial dependence of the Random Forest's CKD probability on its four most influential markers over their observed ranges. The dependence on serum creatinine is a steep step that rises from 0.57 at 0.5 mg/dL to a plateau of 0.77 above approximately 1.3 mg/dL, with no further increase at extreme values. The dependence on specific gravity is a step from 0.80 at 1.015 and below to 0.60 at 1.020 and 0.59 at 1.025. The dependence on packed cell volume falls from 0.68 below 37% to 0.57 above 42%, with its steepest change at 41%, and the dependence on haemoglobin falls from 0.73 below 12 g/dL to 0.54 above 16 g/dL, with its steepest descent at 13.3 g/dL. These breakpoints coincide with clinical reference limits (creatinine above approximately 1.2 mg/dL, haemoglobin below 13 g/dL, haematocrit below approximately 40%, urinary specific gravity of 1.015 or less). They also explain why a linear model with the same markers performs equally well: the dependence is monotone, so a linear logit on the standardised marker approximates the step closely enough for classification even though it cannot reproduce the plateau.

{{FIG:fig11_pdp|Figure 12: Partial Dependence of the Random Forest CKD Probability on Haemoglobin, Serum Creatinine, Urinary Specific Gravity and Packed Cell Volume}}

The SHAP summary (Figure 13) audits the Random Forest at the level of individual patients. Low haemoglobin, high serum creatinine, low specific gravity, low packed cell volume and low red cell count each raise the predicted probability of CKD by up to 0.2, while high values of the same markers lower it by a similar amount; the presence of albuminuria, hypertension or diabetes produces a one-sided positive shift of 0.03 to 0.15. Every direction agrees with the sign of the corresponding Lasso coefficient in Table 9 and with the univariate tests of Section 3.3. The two model families therefore convey the same clinical account through different mechanisms, which constitutes the strongest available evidence that the non-linear learner relies on genuine physiology rather than on artefacts of the data.

### 3.9 Clinical Threshold Optimisation Analysis

Because a missed case of CKD delays treatment that can slow progression to kidney failure, the cost of a false negative exceeds that of a false positive in a screening context. Table 18 reports the holdout sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, MCC and absolute false-negative and false-positive counts of the Lasso and Random Forest models at decision thresholds from 0.05 to 0.95. At the default threshold of 0.50, both models produce one false negative and no false positive. Lowering the Lasso threshold to any value between 0.25 and 0.40 eliminates the false negative without introducing a false positive, giving sensitivity, specificity and MCC of 1.0000. Thresholds below 0.25 begin to admit false positives (4 at 0.20, 13 at 0.10, 22 at 0.05), and thresholds above 0.50 rapidly accumulate false negatives (4 at 0.55, 9 at 0.80, 24 at 0.95). The Random Forest behaves similarly, with a wider optimal window: thresholds from 0.20 to 0.35 classify every holdout patient correctly, thresholds from 0.40 to 0.70 leave the single false negative, and 2 false positives appear at 0.15. For deployment in a screening setting, a decision threshold of approximately 0.30 to 0.35 is therefore recommended for both models; this choice sacrifices nothing on the holdout data and provides a margin of safety on the side of sensitivity. That a Lasso model which ranks every patient correctly nevertheless misses one at 0.50 is a direct consequence of the shrinkage noted in Section 3.7, and it illustrates why threshold tuning on the training data, rather than the conventional 0.5 cut-off, should accompany any regularised classifier (Steyerberg, 2019).

### 3.10 Sensitivity to the Imputation Strategy

Because up to 38% of some markers were imputed, the Lasso and Random Forest pipelines were re-estimated with k-nearest-neighbour imputation (k = 5) and with chained-equation iterative imputation in place of median and mode imputation, with every other element of the protocol held fixed (Table 19). For the Lasso, out-of-fold ROC-AUC across the 30 folds was 0.9996 (SD 0.0020) with median and mode imputation, 0.9975 (SD 0.0070) with k-NN imputation and 0.9995 (SD 0.0022) with iterative imputation (these values use the final tuned hyperparameters and are therefore marginally higher than the nested estimates of Table 12); holdout ROC-AUC was 1.0000, 0.9994 and 0.9988 respectively, holdout accuracy 99.17%, 96.67% and 98.33%, and Brier scores 0.0182, 0.0207 and 0.0208. For the Random Forest, out-of-fold ROC-AUC was 1.0000, 0.9991 (SD 0.0035) and 1.0000, holdout ROC-AUC 1.0000, 0.9997 and 0.9991, holdout accuracy 99.17%, 99.17% and 98.33%, and Brier scores 0.0082, 0.0140 and 0.0146. The simple median and mode strategy was therefore never worse, and usually marginally better, than either model-based alternative, and no imputation choice altered the ranking of the two model families or the conclusion that their discrimination is equivalent. This finding is consistent with the class-dependent missingness profile of Section 3.1. For a CKD patient whose red cell indices were not recorded, model-based imputation predicts those indices from other markers that already encode renal impairment, which adds little beyond the information those markers carry directly, whereas median imputation inserts a near-normal value and relies on the remaining markers.
